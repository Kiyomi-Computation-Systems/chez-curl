Streams

A stream moves a body a piece at a time, so a big download or upload is never held whole. Each level streams both ways.

The examples on this page call the test server: start it first, with node tests/origin.mjs, and give an example the address after "http": in the line it prints, not raw's or h2c's (The examples).

Reading a response as it arrives

At the high level, call-with-response-port makes the call, and hands your procedure the response and a binary input port over its body:

    (call-with-response-port session 'get (string-append base "/pattern/1000000")
      (lambda (response port)
        (display (http-response-status response))
        (newline)
        (let loop ((count 0) (ok #t))
          (let ((piece (get-bytevector-some port)))
            (if (eof-object? piece)
                (begin
                  (display count)
                  (display (if ok " bytes, pattern ok" " bytes, pattern bad"))
                  (newline))
                (loop (+ count (bytevector-length piece))
                      (and ok (pattern? piece count))))))))))
  • The response is its head: status, headers, URL and history. Its body and timings are #f, and http-response-text refuses it: read the port.
  • Your procedure's values are the call's. When it returns or escapes, the port is closed and the transfer cancelled. A Chez engine's expiry during the call is an escape too: once resumed, the port's reads raise that cancel, then Chez's error for a closed port.
  • timeout-ms bounds the whole call, your reading included, as Go's http.Client.Timeout does. A reader slower than the bound meets &http-timeout. Read slowly by design? Pass a longer bound, or #f.
  • stream-buffer sets how much is held, 64 KiB unless you say. A full buffer pauses the transfer.
  • Redirects and retries are decided as each response's head arrives, its status line and headers. A redirect's or a retry's response is read whole into the history. One whose request sent a body is cut off as soon as its head arrives instead, and its history entry's body and timings are #f.
  • A retry that runs out of time having sent nothing hands your procedure the response it retried, with a port over its body, read already. If that response was cut off at its head, the call raises &http-timeout instead.

At the low level, a request's stream-buffer, a size in bytes, makes its token hand you the body in pieces:

(define token
  (curl-submit engine (make-curl-request "https://example.com/big" 'stream-buffer 65536)))

(when (curl-token-head-wait token 10000)     ; the final head came, or the end
  (let ((head (curl-token-head token)))      ; #f when the transfer failed first
    (when head (display (curl-head-status head)))))

(let loop ()
  (let ((piece (curl-token-next-piece token 10000)))
    (when (bytevector? piece)                ; the eof object at the end, #f at the bound
      (loop))))

(curl-token-outcome token)                   ; the response, its body #f, or the failure
  • A 1xx head and a proxy's CONNECT head are passed over: the head is the final one.
  • Each wait answers as data and never raises the transfer's failure: the outcome holds it.
  • curl-token-body-port reads the pieces through a binary input port, made once per token. At the end it answers the eof object, and for a failure it raises the failure, every time.

The rules both levels share:

  • A full buffer pauses the transfer, and reading resumes it. Over HTTP/1.1 the server then waits too. Over HTTP/2, a paused stream that shares its connection with a busy one keeps receiving into libcurl's memory, up to its window. Where no stream shares a connection, see Choosing the HTTP version.
  • The timeout runs while you are not reading.
  • Read with get-bytevector-some or get-u8. When the transfer fails, the read raises its failure, every time after, and a get-bytevector-n, -n! or -all call loses the bytes it had read.
  • The port is its thread's: read and close it on the thread that made it, the call's at the high level. Another thread that tries is refused: at the low level it raises &curl-wrong-thread, and at the high level it is an assertion violation from the call. The one exception is bytes the port had already buffered, which any thread's read takes, so keep reads to the one thread.
  • Closing the port cancels the transfer. A low-level port you never close leaves its transfer paused until its timeout, or its engine's stop.
  • One answer per transfer. A stream is refused beside an Expect header holding 100-continue, whose 417 would make libcurl send the request twice, stream-with-rounds; and beside Digest credentials, digest-with-stream. libcurl's own resend after a dead connection can still make a second request, which brings one head alone.

Sending a body as it is produced

At the high level, a call's body may be a producer: a procedure of no arguments that answers the next bytes, or the eof object at the end. body-length sends its length; without it the body goes chunked over HTTP/1.1, and as plain DATA frames over HTTP/2.

(call-with-http-session
 (lambda (s)
   (call-with-port (open-file-input-port "big.bin")
     (lambda (file)
       (http-put s "https://example.com/big.bin"
                 'body (lambda () (get-bytevector-some file))   ; the eof object ends it
                 'timeout-ms 60000)))))
  • It runs on your thread, only once libcurl asks for the body, and never after it answered the eof object. It answers a bytevector of a byte or more, or the eof object; anything else is an assertion violation.
  • The length is checked. A body short of body-length fails the transfer, body-length-mismatch; a piece past it raises that reason as &http-request-error, before any of it is sent.
  • It is sent at most once. Once libcurl has asked for it, no retry takes the call, and a redirect that would resend it raises body-not-replayable.
  • Its raise leaves the call as raised, once the transfer is cancelled.
  • Through call-with-response-port, the port feeds the producer while you read, so a server that answers as the body arrives gets the rest. It feeds only when no bytes are waiting: a server that sends faster than you read holds the upload back, and a procedure that never reads, and never returns, starves it. A producer's raise inside the port's read sticks: every later read raises it. A producer that reads its own port is refused.
  • A request group takes no producer: go and http-parallel refuse one, stream-in-group.

At the low level, a request's body 'stream makes its token take the body from your puts, into a buffer of 64 KiB. body-length sends its length; #f sends it chunked over HTTP/1.1, as plain DATA frames over HTTP/2. A body-length beside a body that does not stream is refused, body-length-without-stream.

(define token
  (curl-submit engine
               (make-curl-request (string-append base "/sink?key=example-upload")
                                  'method 'put 'body 'stream 'body-length size
                                  'timeout-ms 10000)))

;; Each put waits while the token's buffer is full; the end says the body
;; is whole.
(let loop ((at 0))
  (when (< at size)
    (let ((count (min 65536 (- size at))))
      (curl-token-put-piece! token (piece at count))
      (loop (+ at count)))))
(curl-token-end-body! token)
  • A put waits while the buffer is full. Give it a bound, in milliseconds, and it answers #f when the bound passes. curl-token-room-wait answers #t once libcurl has asked for the body and there is room.
  • done means the transfer has ended: a put that answers done took nothing, and curl-token-outcome tells how it ended.
  • The length is checked. An end short of body-length, or a piece past it, fails the transfer, body-length-mismatch; the put past it raises.
  • curl-token-body-count tells what your puts committed to the buffer, not what reached the server.

The rules both levels share:

  • It is sent at most once, on a fresh connection. A piece handed to libcurl is gone, so a resend libcurl wants after that, for a dead connection or a refused HTTP/2 stream, fails the transfer, body-not-replayable. The upload never reuses a pooled connection: each opens one, and over TLS a handshake. Once it ends, a later transfer may reuse that connection.
  • The timeout runs while you are not putting.
  • An early answer ends the upload. A server may answer, a 413 say, before the body is sent; then every later put answers done. When the transfer ends, and whether libcurl still asks for the rest, varies by libcurl, as does whether an early 200 can end it as a success with the body cut. A server that must have the whole body should say so in its answer.
  • Over HTTP/2, a transfer that streams both ways sends only while you read the response's pieces: else the upload stops at the peer's window.
  • Over HTTP/2, pass a timeout-ms: on some libcurls an answer that comes before the body is sent whole never ends the transfer.

What varies by libcurl has each of these by version.
